Does the rise of Asia's universities pass Times Higher Education's test?

Times Higher Education (THE) has just released its World University Rankings 2027, months after Asian universities emerged among the biggest climbers in the QS rankings. THE weighs teaching and research differently. We look into whether Asia’s rise holds up under its methodology, and why Singapore and Hong Kong tell different stories when the two rankings disagree.

On Sep 30, 2026, **Times Higher Education** released its World University Rankings 2027. **Oxford** holds first place for an 11th consecutive year. The result at the top matters less than the movement below it, and whether Asia’s QS climb survives a ranking that puts less weight on reputation.

The QS 2027 rankings, released in June, showed universities in Hong Kong and mainland China climbing while several Western institutions slipped. THE uses a different [methodology](https://graphics.businesstimes.com.sg/specials/universities/index.html), placing more weight on teaching and research quality and less on reputation surveys. If Asia’s rise is genuine, it should also show up in THE’s table. If it does not, part of the gain may reflect how QS measures reputation.

## The balance of power · THE Top 50

The **Times Higher Education** rankings spread their weight more evenly across teaching, research environment and research quality. QS, by contrast, leans heavily on reputation surveys. The THE Top 50 is therefore a useful test of how far Asia’s rise extends.

A decade ago, the Top 50 was overwhelmingly Western. In **2018**, only **six Asian universities** made the list, while North America alone held about half of the places.

By **2024**, Asia’s share had grown. Universities in mainland China, Hong Kong, Singapore and Japan moved further up the table as their research output and citation impact rose.

In the **2026** table, **10 Asian universities** were in THE’s Top 50, nearly double the 2018 count, while North America’s share had declined slightly.

In the **2027** table, Asia still holds **10 of the Top 50**, unchanged from 2026. That is a plateau, not another leap. The decade rise is real. What 2027 asks more sharply is which parts of Asia pass THE’s test.

## Who THE favours · 2026 to 2027

The chart shows the top tier moving from **THE 2026** to **THE 2027**. Each mortarboard represents a university, and its colour marks its region.

At the top, **Oxford** holds first place for an 11th consecutive year. The US and UK universities mostly shuffle within the top 10: Stanford rises to join Princeton in third, while Cambridge slips to fifth.

Within Asia, the picture splits. In Singapore, the **National University of Singapore** climbs from 17th to 15th, while **Nanyang Technological University** rises from 31st to 28th. Both move up on THE.

Hong Kong tells a different story. **HKU** holds at 33rd and **CUHK** slips from 41st to 42nd. On THE, neither repeats the climb that QS recorded earlier this year.

On the mainland, **Tsinghua** rises from 12th to 11th, ahead of ETH Zurich, while **Peking** holds at 13th. THE’s research-weighted measures continue to favour these two.

One year cannot settle whether Asia is rising. But THE’s movers draw a clearer line: Singapore climbed, the mainland held near the top, and Hong Kong did not advance.

## Where the rankings disagree · THE vs QS

Asian universities can rank higher in one system than in another. The chart shows the gap between each university’s **THE 2027 rank and QS 2027 rank**. Universities to the left of the centre line rank better on THE, while those to the right rank better on QS.

The gap is widest in Hong Kong. **HKU** ranks 11th on QS but 33rd on THE, a difference of 22 places. **CUHK** ranks 18th on QS and 42nd on THE. If Asia’s rise were mainly a reputation story, this is the pattern you would expect.

Singapore sits closer to the centre. **NUS** (QS 10 / THE 15) and **NTU** (QS 12 / THE 28) still rank better on QS, but NUS’s gap has narrowed to five places, far smaller than Hong Kong’s.

Mainland China’s research-intensive universities now sit close on both. **Tsinghua** ranks 11th on THE and 14th on QS, while **Peking** ranks 13th on both.

Several Western research universities also rank higher on THE. **Princeton**, **Berkeley** and **Yale** still rank higher on THE than on QS, while **Imperial College London**, which benefits from QS’ reputation weighting, still shows the opposite pattern.

Whether Asian universities are rising therefore depends on which ranking is used, and on which city is in view. QS places heavy weight on reputation. THE weighs teaching and research differently. In 2027, that gap is largest in Hong Kong, narrower in Singapore, and smallest on the mainland.

*(Chart: Diverging lollipops — THE − QS gap by university, coloured by region)*

## So how should you read Asia’s rise?

Asia’s presence in THE’s Top 50 has nearly doubled since 2018, which shows that the rise is real. But 2027 is a hold, not a breakthrough, and the year matters most for what it reveals inside Asia. Singapore climbed on THE. Mainland China’s flagships remain strong on both systems. Hong Kong universities still rank highly on QS and merely solid on THE.

As with any league table, the most useful approach is to compare rankings rather than rely on one. Asia’s gains largely hold on a ranking that does not rely mainly on reputation, but the sharper lesson is local. QS crowned Hong Kong, while THE tells a more favourable story for Singapore.
